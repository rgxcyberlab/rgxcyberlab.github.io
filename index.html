Healthcare threat surface

the part I know from the inside

Hospital networks are a genuinely hard target class: life-critical uptime, decades of legacy protocol, medical devices nobody is allowed to patch mid-shift, and data that cannot simply be rotated after a breach. These are the surfaces I study.

HL7 v2 interfaces

Critical

Clinical messaging still commonly rides MLLP over plaintext TCP with no authentication between integrated systems. Anything on the interface segment can read or forge orders and results.

DICOM / PACS exposure

Critical

Imaging archives have a long public history of sitting internet-reachable with anonymous access enabled. Studies carry full patient identifiers inside the file header, not just the pixels.

Unpatchable medical devices

High

Embedded and regulated devices run frozen OS images for their certified lifetime. Segmentation and monitoring are the only real controls, which makes network visibility the whole game.

Shared clinical workstations

High

Speed of care pushes wards toward shared logins and long sessions. Attribution collapses, and credential reuse becomes the shortest path from a ward PC to the record system.

Reporting & export paths

Medium

My own daily territory. Analytics extracts leave the clinical system and land in spreadsheets, mail and shared drives, where PHI quietly outlives every control that governed the source.

Ransomware blast radius

Critical

In hospitals the impact metric is not downtime cost, it is diverted ambulances and delayed procedures. Recovery planning has to be measured against clinical continuity, not RTO alone.

Engagement methodology

the sequence I work to

A real assessment is a process, not a tool run. These phases are ordered because each one depends on the last, and because the last phase is the only one the client actually pays for.

01

Scope & authorization

Written permission, defined in-scope ranges, testing windows, escalation contacts and explicit exclusions. In a clinical environment this also means agreeing which systems are never touched during patient hours.

artifacts: rules of engagement, scope document, emergency contact chain

02

Reconnaissance

Building a picture of the target's exposed footprint before touching anything: domains, subdomains, public records, exposed services, vendor relationships and the technology stack that follows from them.

tooling: dns enumeration, certificate transparency, osint sources, search operators

03

Enumeration & vulnerability identification

Mapping live hosts, open ports, service versions and application surfaces, then correlating those against known weaknesses and misconfigurations. Verifying findings by hand, because scanner output is a hypothesis rather than a finding.

tooling: nmap, nikto, burp suite, nessus-class scanners, manual verification

04

Controlled exploitation

Proving impact within the agreed scope: confirming a weakness is genuinely reachable and what it actually exposes. Least-damaging proof wins, and anything destructive stays out unless the scope explicitly buys it.

discipline: minimum necessary access, no data exfiltration, full activity logging

05

Detection review

The half most portfolios skip. Going back through the defender's telemetry to ask which of my actions generated a signal, which passed silently, and what rule would have caught the difference.

focus: log coverage gaps, alert tuning, MITRE ATT&CK mapping

06

Reporting & remediation

Findings written for two audiences at once: an executive summary that survives a board meeting, and technical detail an engineer can reproduce and fix. Risk rated against clinical impact, not just CVSS.

deliverables: findings report, prioritized remediation plan, retest

Rules of engagement

non-negotiable
  1. Written authorization or lab only. Nothing gets tested without documented permission from someone empowered to give it, or inside a range I own outright. Curiosity is not authorization.
  2. Never real patient data. Every dataset in this lab is synthetic or fully de-identified. Healthcare has one breach that cannot be undone, and a training exercise is not worth it.
  3. Minimum necessary access. Prove the finding, then stop. No lateral movement past scope, no data pulled out, no destructive proof where a screenshot settles it.
  4. Responsible disclosure. Anything found outside a formal engagement goes to the owner privately, with a reasonable window to fix it, before it is written about anywhere.
  5. Clinical safety outranks the test. In a live hospital environment, any sign that testing could touch patient care stops the engagement immediately. Nothing found is worth a delayed procedure.
